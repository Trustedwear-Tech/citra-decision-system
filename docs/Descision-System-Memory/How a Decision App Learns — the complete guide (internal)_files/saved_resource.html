How a Decision App learns from your officers

A complete, plain-language guide to the memory system: what it stores, how a lesson is formed, what can stop a lesson from forming, how the app decides which lessons apply to which cases, and who controls all of it.

Read this if

You work on, sell, demo, support or build Decision Apps and you want to actually understand the memory — not just repeat phrases about it. No prior knowledge is assumed. Every term is defined the first time it appears, and nothing is left as "the system handles it".

This describes what is built and running as of 7 August 2026, not a roadmap. Where a number is configurable, the default is given. If this guide and the software ever disagree, the software is right and this guide needs fixing.

Part one · Why this exists

Chapter 1The job the app is doing all day

Picture a motor insurance claims team. Every day a few hundred claims arrive. Each one has to be looked at by a claims officer, who decides: approve it, reject it, ask for more documents, or send it for investigation.

A Decision App sits in front of that work. For each claim it reads the record and any attached photos and documents, and it writes a recommendation — "approve this", "ask for the police report first", "send this one to investigation" — with its reasoning. An officer then looks at the recommendation and either agrees with it or changes it.

The officer is always the one who decides. The app proposes; a person disposes. That does not change as the app gets better, and nothing in this guide ever gives the app more authority.

Now, the interesting part. Over a year, that claims team accumulates an enormous amount of practical knowledge that is written down nowhere. Not in the policy document, not in the standard operating procedure — in people's heads. Things like: a theft claim on a policy bought three weeks ago, with no police report attached, is worth a second look no matter how clean the paperwork is.

That knowledge is the thing we are trying to capture. Not to replace the official rules — to capture the part the official rules never managed to write down.

Why bother at all

If the app never learns, every officer correction is spent once and thrown away. The same mistake gets corrected by the same person next week, and the week after. Worse, when an experienced officer retires, everything they knew leaves with them. The memory system's whole purpose is to make an officer's correction count more than once.

Chapter 2The memory we had before, and why it failed

The first version of this worked in a way that sounds sensible until you watch it run. The app kept one paragraph of learned guidance per app — a page of text describing what officers had taught it. Every time an officer rejected a recommendation, that officer's reason was added to the paragraph, and an AI model was asked to rewrite the whole paragraph to fit everything in under a thousand words.

Three things went wrong, and all three are fatal.

It diluted

Rewriting the same paragraph over and over is a game of telephone. A sharp, specific lesson — "on theft claims over ₹25 lakh with no police report, require the report before approval" — gets rewritten into "verify supporting documentation on high-value claims", which gets rewritten into "ensure documentation is appropriate". After twenty rewrites, nothing in the paragraph means anything. The most valuable lessons were the most specific ones, and specificity was exactly what the rewriting destroyed.

It applied everything to everything

There was one paragraph for the whole app. A lesson learned from theft claims was read by the app when it looked at a windshield claim, because there was no way to say "this applies to theft claims only". So the paragraph was simultaneously too vague to be useful and too broad to be safe.

You could not ask it why

If a supervisor read a line in the paragraph and asked "who taught it that, and from which case?", there was no answer. The original corrections had been melted into prose. You could not check a lesson, you could not remove one lesson without rewriting the paragraph, and you could not tell a good lesson from a bad one because nothing was tracked separately.

And one operational problem

The rewrite happened inside the officer's request. Every rejection paid for a full AI rewrite before the screen came back. Learning was making the officer's day slower.

Chapter 3The idea that replaced it

The replacement is one idea, applied consistently: stop keeping one big blob, and start keeping many small, separate, labelled lessons.

Each lesson is a single sentence. Each sentence carries three things with it:

  • Who taught it and from which cases — the actual officer corrections it was built from, kept forever, viewable on screen.
  • Which kinds of case it applies to — so a theft lesson is never read on a windshield claim.
  • How it has performed — how often it was used, and how often the officer overruled the app on a case where it was used.

Because lessons are separate, none of them dilute each other. Because each is labelled, the app can pick out only the ones that fit the case in front of it. Because each keeps its evidence, a supervisor can always ask "why does it think that?" and get a real answer. And because each is tracked separately, a bad lesson can be retired without touching the good ones.

That is the entire design. Everything in the rest of this guide is detail about how a lesson gets made, checked, chosen and controlled.

Chapter 4Rules and judgements — the most important distinction in this guide

If you remember one thing from this document, remember this: the rules are the SOP, and the SOP always wins. What officers teach is judgement, and judgement never overrides a rule.

Definition — the SOP

The organisation's standard operating procedure: its written policy documents, circulars and manuals. The app reads these live from the department's document library at the moment it works on a case. Nobody learns them, nobody rewrites them, and nothing the app picks up from officers is allowed to contradict them.

Definition — a judgement

A single sentence the app has learned from officers correcting it. It covers the ground the SOP does not spell out — the practical experience an officer applies without being told to.

The clearest example is from lending, not insurance. A loan application arrives. The declared earnings look healthy, and every box the SOP asks about is ticked. But an experienced officer notices that the tax filings do not corroborate those earnings at some of the stated identifiers, and rejects it.

There is no SOP clause that says "check whether tax filings corroborate declared earnings at the stated identifiers". No policy document enumerates that tell. It is the officer's eye — accumulated experience about how a certain kind of misstatement looks. That is judgement, and it is exactly the thing the memory captures.

So there are three levels of authority, in a strict order:

Level 1 · Rules

The SOP. Supreme. If a learned judgement ever conflicts with the SOP, the app follows the SOP and reports the conflict. Nothing learned can soften, reinterpret or outrank it.

▲ never overridden by ▼
Level 2 · Team judgement

A lesson three or more different officers have independently taught. Shown to the app as an established practice of this team — still underneath the SOP.

▲ outranks ▼
Level 3 · Individual judgement

A lesson one officer has taught. Used from the very first day, but honestly labelled as one person's view so the app — and the next officer — knows to weigh it rather than obey it.

Why one officer's view is used at all

An earlier design held back any lesson until three officers had agreed. That sounded prudent and was actually a bug: a branch office with one officer, or a new app with two users, would never learn anything at all, and nobody would be told why. Meanwhile the real risk — "one person's opinion quietly becomes company policy" — is not solved by hiding the lesson. It is solved by saying whose opinion it is. So a single officer's judgement is used immediately and labelled clearly, and it starts being measured from its first outing, which means a bad one is caught quickly rather than never.

You will see this distinction enforced in the product's language everywhere: the memory screen is titled "Judgements it has learned", never "rules"; the batch job says it folds corrections "into the judgements your apps apply — your SOP stays the rules, and always wins". This is not branding. Calling learned material "rules" invites people to trust it the way they trust policy, and it is not policy.

Part two · The five things everything is built from

Chapter 5The case

A case is one unit of work: one claim, one loan application, one inspection report, one complaint. It is a record in the customer's own system — their claims database, their billing system — that the app reads through a governed connection. The app never owns that record and, in the memory context, never changes it.

Everything else in this guide hangs off the case: the app makes a recommendation about a case, an officer corrects that recommendation on a case, and a judgement applies to certain kinds of case.

Chapter 6Case labels: how the app describes what kind of case this is

This is the concept most people find unfamiliar, so we will take it slowly. It is also the concept that makes everything else work.

Suppose an officer teaches the app something about a theft claim. Should the app remember that lesson for every claim it ever sees afterwards? Obviously not — a lesson about theft claims should not change how a cracked windscreen is handled. But how does software know that one claim is "a theft claim over ₹25 lakh with no police report" and another is "a small windscreen claim"?

It knows because every case gets stamped with a short list of labels, worked out automatically from the case's own data.

Definition — case labels

A short list of plain tags describing the kind of case this is, calculated from the record's own fields at the moment the app looks at it. Nobody types them; they are derived. In the software these are called facets, and on screen they appear as small grey chips underneath each judgement.

Example — one claim, its labels

A ₹38 lakh theft claim, filed on a policy that started three weeks ago, with no police report number filled in, on a personal (not fleet) policy, where the fraud photo-check did not flag anything:

loss_type: theft amount_band: 25000_100000 police_report: absent policy_class: personal policy_age: lt_30 photo_check: clear

Six labels. That is the app's description of "what kind of case is this". A judgement that was learned about theft claims in that amount range will apply here. A judgement learned about windscreen claims will not.

Where the labels come from

They are read off the columns the app is already bound to, in five simple shapes:

ShapeWhat it doesExample label
A fixed listA column that can only hold a few known values — claim type, category, class.loss_type: theft
A rangeA number turned into a band rather than kept exact.amount_band: 25000_100000
Filled or emptyWhether a piece of evidence is present at all.police_report: absent
An ageThe gap between two dates, banded.policy_age: lt_30
A check resultWhether one of the platform's fraud screens fired on this case.photo_check: fired
Why ranges instead of exact amounts

If the label were amount: 3,842,150, no two cases would ever share it, and no lesson could ever be said to apply to "claims like this one". Bands are what make cases comparable. The same logic applies to dates: "policy less than 30 days old" is a meaningful category; "policy started on 4 July" is not.

Who decides which labels exist

The person building the app decides, once, at build time. When a business analyst describes the app in plain English, the builder proposes a small vocabulary: which of the dataset's columns become labels. That vocabulary is saved with the app. (Until August 2026 the builder also authored a list of reject-reason categories; that list is gone — see chapter 7.)

Two deliberate constraints:

  • Four to eight label families, not thirty. The question for each candidate is not "do we know this?" but "would an officer's correction ever depend on it?" The channel a complaint arrived through almost never changes why a decision was wrong, so it should not be a label. Extra labels cost real money: they make cases look less alike, which makes lessons slower to form.
  • The possible values must be declared. If a case arrives with a value nobody declared, it is labelled __unknown and counted as drift. That is how the team finds out the source system started using a new category. An undeclared label can never be used to scope a judgement — an alarm must stay an alarm.

Chapter 7The correction: what an officer actually does

The app shows a recommendation. The officer disagrees. What happens next is the raw material for everything.

The officer is asked for one thing:

  1. One sentence in their own words saying what was actually wrong — at least ten words, checked on the server and not only in the browser, because the embedded card runs on the customer's own page and a rule that lives only in a browser is a suggestion.
Why there is no category to pick

Until August 2026 the officer also chose a category from a short list, and this guide argued it was essential: free text cannot be grouped reliably, so a fixed handle was needed before anyone read a word of the officer's sentence. That reasoning was wrong in practice, and the taxonomy has been removed.

The failure was that a why ended up choosing a where. Grouping partitioned on the category first, so two officers correcting identical cases were never compared at all if they happened to pick different labels. Each pile then fell below the size floor and both lessons were silently dropped. The list caused a second, more visible problem: it was the app's reject-reason list, so an officer overriding a rejection towards approval was offered a menu of reasons to decline — labels that made no sense for the decision being made.

Grouping now uses evidence the system observed rather than a label a human chose: corrections are partitioned by which fields the officer actually changed, and then compared on their case labels and their wording. Nobody picks anything. The ten-word floor exists because that sentence now carries all of the substance.

Definition — a correction

One officer's disagreement with one recommendation, stored as: who, when, which case, the case's labels frozen as they were at that moment, the category they picked, their sentence, and any fields they changed. Corrections are append-only — they are never edited and never deleted, by anyone, including us.

Two details matter more than they look:

The labels are frozen onto the correction. If the claim is edited next month, or the app's label vocabulary changes next year, the correction still remembers the kind of case it was about. Evidence that changes under you is not evidence.

Agreement is never recorded as support. When an officer simply approves a recommendation, that teaches the app nothing. This is deliberate: if approvals counted, an app that produced plausible-looking output would accumulate "support" from officers clicking through under time pressure, and rubber-stamping would write policy. Only a correction — an actual disagreement — teaches.

Chapter 8The judgement: one sentence the app has learned

A judgement is the finished product: one sentence, at most about forty words, phrased in the officers' own vocabulary.

Example — a real one, from the dev system

"Classify a cracked bushing with visible oil seepage as high severity, never medium."

defect_type: bushing_crack oil_leak: present

Team judgement · 3 officers · built from three separate corrections, each viewable underneath it on the memory screen.

Everything a judgement carries:

It carriesMeaning
The sentenceWritten once when the lesson first formed, and never rewritten afterwards.
Its labelsThe kinds of case it applies to. Empty means "every case this app sees".
Its evidenceLinks to the exact corrections that taught it, and the names of the officers who made them.
Its standingIndividual (one officer) or team (three or more), plus any special state such as suspended or held.
Its track recordHow many times it was put in front of the app, and how often the officer overruled the app on those cases.
Part three · How a judgement is made

Chapter 9The learning batch: the background job that does the thinking

Corrections pile up as officers work. Turning them into judgements is done by a scheduled background job we call the learning batch.

Definition — the learning batch

A job that runs quietly inside the Decision App service on a timer, looks for corrections that have not been folded into judgements yet, and folds the ones that are ready. It is not a person, not a nightly export, and not something an officer waits for.

When it runs

The job wakes on a timer — every 15 minutes by default — and asks a simple question about each group of pending corrections: is this group ready? A group is ready when either of two things is true:

  • It has five or more corrections waiting, or
  • Its oldest waiting correction is more than six hours old.

The first condition handles a busy app: enough evidence has arrived, fold it now. The second handles a quiet one: a small team that produces two corrections a week should not wait months for a fifth. Either way, learning happens the same day.

Why a batch and not "learn immediately"

Three reasons, in order of importance. Speed for the officer: the old design ran an AI rewrite inside the rejection request, so the person doing the work paid for the learning. Now the officer's screen returns instantly and the thinking happens later. Evidence needs company: a single correction on its own often cannot tell you whether a lesson is real or a one-off; waiting a few minutes lets related corrections arrive and be considered together. Correct counting: the job elects a single leader, so when the service is running several copies of itself, only one of them folds corrections. Otherwise two copies could each count the same officer's correction and a lesson would falsely look corroborated.

What a run does, and what it reports

Each run reports what it did: how many groups it looked at, how many judgements it created, how many existing judgements it strengthened, and how many groups it skipped. Administrators see this live on the Learning Batch screen, along with how many corrections are waiting and which app they belong to.

Two controls sit there:

  • Pause — stops learning, nothing else. Officers keep working, the app keeps recommending, corrections keep being recorded. When you resume, everything that accumulated is folded in. Pausing loses nothing.
  • Fold feedback now — runs a pass immediately and ignores both readiness thresholds. This is what you use in a demo, or when you want a lesson visible before a meeting.

One quiet detail worth knowing: a deployment can have a test environment alongside the live one, and the batch deliberately sweeps both. An earlier version swept only live, which meant apps under test accumulated corrections that were never folded and their memory sat empty while looking merely "not learned yet".

Chapter 10Grouping: what a "cluster" is, in plain words

The batch cannot look at a hundred corrections and write one sentence. It has to first work out which corrections are about the same thing. That sorting step is called clustering, and a cluster is simply one pile of corrections that appear to be making the same point.

Definition — a cluster

A group of officer corrections that the batch has decided are about the same lesson. One cluster produces at most one judgement. If the corrections cannot be grouped into a pile, no judgement is written from them — they simply stay waiting.

Two corrections land in the same pile only if all three of these are true.

Test one — the same reason category

This is an absolute wall, not a preference. A correction categorised "evidence insufficient" can never be grouped with one categorised "amount incorrect", however similar their wording. Two officers can be looking at the same claim and be unhappy about entirely different things; merging those would produce a sentence that misrepresents both of them.

There is one accommodation: if the app's builder renames a category later, the old name is kept as an alias, so corrections made before and after the rename are still recognised as the same category. A rename must not split one lesson into two.

Test two — similar wording

The corrections must actually use overlapping language. We compare the meaningful words in each sentence — ignoring filler — and require a real degree of overlap.

Why word overlap and not an AI deciding "these mean the same thing"

Because we would then be trusting a language model to decide what officers meant, and a model that is feeling generous will merge two lessons that are not the same lesson — producing a judgement neither officer would sign. Word overlap is dumb, predictable, and reviewable: you can look at two corrections and see for yourself why they were or were not grouped. We accept that this is conservative. A lesson that fails to group is delayed; a lesson that groups wrongly is wrong.

Test three — comparable kinds of case

The corrections must have come from similar cases, judged by the case labels frozen onto each of them. This is where the third test can get subtly wrong, so it is worth explaining exactly how it is measured.

The natural approach — count the labels two cases share, divide by the total number of distinct labels between them — has a nasty side effect. The more detail an app records about its cases, the more chances two cases have to differ on something irrelevant, and the lower that score gets. An app that describes its cases richly would learn more slowly than a careless one. That is backwards.

So instead we ask a different question: of the labels these two cases could possibly have shared, how many did they actually share? We compare against the smaller of the two label sets, and require at least half.

Example — why this matters

Two corrections about the same underlying lesson. Case A carries six labels, case B carries six. They agree on the three that matter (loss_type: theft, amount_band, police_report: absent) and differ on three incidental ones (channel, region, status).

The naive method: three shared out of nine distinct = 0.33. Below the bar — not grouped, lesson never learned.

The method we use: three shared out of a possible six = 0.5. At the bar — grouped, lesson learned.

One more accommodation: if either side has no labels at all — which happens with history imported from before the app had a label vocabulary — the test passes. Absence of information is not disagreement. Without this, every piece of migrated history would have been permanently unable to corroborate anything an officer said afterwards.

What happens to a cluster that matches something we already know

Before writing anything new, the batch checks whether an existing judgement already says this. It compares against the fingerprint of meaningful words stored on each judgement when it was written. If it matches, the batch does not write a new sentence and does not rewrite the old one. It records that these officers said it too — adding their names, raising the support count, and possibly promoting the judgement from individual to team. Strengthening a judgement uses no AI at all; it is pure bookkeeping.

Chapter 11The quality gates: what stops a bad lesson, and what a refusal costs

Before a cluster is allowed to become a judgement, it passes a series of checks. These are the quality gates. This chapter answers the three questions people always ask about them: who runs them, when they run, and whether refusing something means we lost it.

A gate added in August 2026, and the failure that bought it

A judgement can be perfectly formed and still never fire, if the label it is scoped to can never be computed. On the lending app, the review screen's column list did not include sourcing_channel. Labels are derived from the columns the screen actually loads — so every case came out labelled sourcing_channel: __unknown, and the judgement scoped to sourcing_channel: dsa was unreachable by anything, for any case.

It fired on 19 of 19 cases when the experiment called the service directly with the whole record, and on 0 of 1 through the screen officers actually use. The only symptom was a drift warning that looked entirely routine. Publishing now refuses an app whose label reads a column no screen loads, and the runtime separates "this column is absent altogether" — a wiring fault that disables every judgement in that family at once — from "this value was not declared", which is ordinary drift. They need different fixes and used to share one message.

Who runs the gates, and when

The learning batch runs them, automatically, on every cluster, before a judgement is written. No human is involved and no human is asked to approve anything. Officers never see a gate; they are not interrupted, warned, or blocked at the moment they write their correction. The gates run minutes later, in the background, out of everyone's way.

Gate 1 — is there actually a lesson in here?

Some corrections carry no content. "As discussed." "See file." "Not ok." Three officers writing "as discussed" on similar cases will happily cluster together — they share a category and their words overlap perfectly — and would produce a content-free judgement with three officers' names on it, injected into every comparable case forever.

So a cluster must contain a minimum amount of substance — at least six meaningful words across the whole pile, ignoring filler. Below that, no judgement is written.

Gate 2 — is this about a pattern, or about a person?

Officers sometimes write "Mr Sharma is a fraudster" or quote an account number. If that becomes a judgement, the app carries a permanent, unreviewed allegation about a named individual into every future case — which is both wrong and a liability.

So the written sentence is checked for personal names, honorific-plus-name patterns, and long identifier numbers. If any appear, the sentence is rejected and rewritten once, with the offending fragment quoted back and an explicit instruction to describe the recurring pattern instead. Often that succeeds: "Mr Sharma is a fraudster" becomes something like "treat repeat claimants with prior fraud findings as requiring verification". If the second attempt still names someone, nothing is written.

Concerns about specific people are not lost — they belong to entity screening, which is a different mechanism designed for exactly that and subject to its own controls. They just do not belong in a lesson that fires on everybody.

Gate 3 — are these officers actually agreeing?

Two corrections can share a category and share vocabulary while pulling in opposite directions: one officer moves a severity from medium up to high, another moves it from high down to medium. Averaging those would produce a confident sentence that neither officer believes.

So a cluster whose corrections push the same field in opposite directions is split, not averaged. Each side goes back to waiting for corroboration on its own terms. If one side later accumulates agreement and the other does not, the supported side becomes a judgement and the other simply doesn't.

Gate 4 — did the sentence get written at all?

Writing the sentence is the one place an AI model is used. If that call fails — the model is down, the response is unusable — nothing is written and nothing is marked as done. The next run tries again.

The honest failure rule

There is no fallback sentence. A placeholder like "officers have raised concerns about this case type" would become a permanent judgement with real officers' names attached to words they never said. Failing loudly and retrying is always better than inventing something plausible.

So does refusing cost us the correction?

This is the important question, and the answer is no — a refused correction is never deleted, never edited, and never marked as finished. It stays exactly where it was, waiting, and every future run of the batch reconsiders it.

Concretely, here is what happens to a correction the gates refused:

  • It stays in the evidence ledger permanently. That ledger is append-only. Nothing in the system deletes from it.
  • It is reconsidered on every run. Corrections are marked as consumed only after a judgement has been successfully written from them. A refusal marks nothing.
  • It can still corroborate. A correction that was too brief on its own still counts as a real officer disagreeing about a real case. When a colleague later writes a fuller sentence about the same thing, the brief one joins that cluster and helps it reach team standing.
  • Somebody is told. Corrections refused for being too brief are flagged, counted, and shown on the memory screen: "N correction(s) were too brief to learn from — ask officers for one concrete sentence." That is a coaching signal, and it is the only way anyone would ever know.
Why we would rather refuse than accept

The two mistakes are not equally expensive. A lesson that forms late costs us some delay, and we can see it coming: the evidence is sitting there, counted, on a screen. A lesson that forms wrongly is put in front of the app on every comparable case from that moment on, quietly nudging decisions, and the only way we find out is by watching officers overrule it for weeks. One is a visible delay; the other is invisible damage. When in doubt, we take the delay.

Chapter 12Writing the sentence — once, and never again

When a cluster passes the gates, an AI model is asked to do one narrow job: phrase, in a single sentence of about forty words, the lesson these officers have already stated. It is given their actual words and told to stay inside them. It is not asked to reason about the domain, invent policy, or improve on what they said.

And then that sentence is fixed for life. It is never rewritten. When more officers make the same correction, the batch adds their names, raises the support count, extends the evidence list, and possibly promotes the judgement to team standing — but does not touch a single word.

Why never rewrite

Two reasons. The first is the dilution problem from chapter 2 — repeated rewriting is what destroyed the old memory, and the only reliable cure is to write once. The second is honesty: a judgement is displayed alongside the exact corrections that produced it. If we rewrote the sentence as new evidence arrived, it would gradually drift away from the words underneath it, and a supervisor reading the evidence would find that it does not support the claim. A judgement must always be something the people underneath it would recognise as their own.

If a judgement genuinely needs different words, it is retired and a new one forms from the evidence. That is a visible, attributable event — not an invisible edit.

Chapter 13Deciding how far a judgement reaches

A new judgement needs to know which cases it applies to. This is worked out from the cases it was learned from, in two steps.

Step one — what did these cases have in common?

Take the labels of every case in the cluster and keep only the ones all of them share. Not the combined list — the shared list. If three corrections came from theft claims, two of which were on personal policies and one on a fleet policy, then "theft" survives and "personal policy" does not.

Why the shared list, not the combined one

The combined list would produce a judgement scoped to a set of labels no single case actually has, so it would apply to almost nothing. The shared list is the honest answer to "what did the cases we learned from have in common?"

Step two — drop the labels that tell us nothing

Some labels are shared by nearly every case the app ever sees. In a single-country deployment, every case is country: us. Scoping a judgement to "US cases" is not narrowing anything — it is noise that makes the scope look meaningful when it isn't.

So each surviving label is checked against how common it is across the app's cases generally. A label carried by 95% of cases anyway is dropped. A label carried by 12% of cases is genuinely telling us something, and stays. When there are too few cases for that comparison to mean anything, the check is skipped and the shared list is used as-is — a judgement that comes out too narrow can be widened later, whereas one that comes out too broad starts misfiring immediately.

The result is the small chip row you see under each judgement on the memory screen. A judgement with no chips is not a bug; it means the lesson genuinely applies to every case this app handles.

Chapter 14One officer, then the team

A judgement's standing depends on how many different officers have taught it. Not how many corrections — how many distinct people. Five corrections from one officer is still one officer's view.

StandingReached whenHow the app is told
Individual judgementOne or two distinct officers"one officer's judgement — not yet corroborated by the team; weigh it and verify against the record before relying on it"
Team judgementThree or more distinct officers (configurable per app)"team judgement — 3 officers"

Two safeguards keep individual judgements in their place:

  • At most three individual judgements are shown on any one case. Uncorroborated opinions do not get to crowd out the corroborated ones.
  • A team judgement shadows an individual one that covers the same ground. The established practice is what the app sees; the individual echo is dropped rather than shown twice.

When officers disagree with each other

Sometimes a judgement attracts genuine dissent — later officers correcting in the opposite direction. Once dissent reaches about a third of the total feedback on that judgement, it stops being asserted at all. It is not deleted and not hidden; it is presented to the app as an open question: "officers disagree on this — surface both readings." A supervisor decides.

This applies to a lone judgement facing a single objection too: one officer taught it, one officer disagrees, and until somebody breaks the tie the app is told there is a disagreement rather than being handed one side of it.

Chapter 15Checking every judgement against the SOP

Chapter 4 said the SOP always wins. Here is the machinery that makes that true.

Whenever a judgement is created — and again whenever it is strengthened — it is checked against the app's live SOP. The relevant policy text is fetched from the department's document library and compared with the judgement, asking one narrow question: does the SOP contradict this?

If it does, the judgement is suspended. It stops being asserted immediately. The app is told only that a learned judgement conflicts with the SOP here — follow the SOP, so it knows there is an unresolved tension without being handed the disputed advice.

The two-tap resolution

A supervisor opens the judgement on the memory screen and sees the conflict explained, with two buttons:

A

"SOP is right — retire it"

The officers' habit is out of date. The judgement is retired. Its evidence stays on file, so if officers keep making the same correction the disagreement resurfaces rather than disappearing.

B

"Officers are right — SOP needs updating"

The practice is correct and the written procedure has fallen behind. The judgement returns to service, and the disagreement is recorded as the organisation's own signal that a policy document needs revising.

Why both directions are one tap

Because both happen. The SOP is authoritative, but it is also a document written by people at a point in time, and reality moves. If the only available answer were "retire the judgement", the system would quietly destroy the evidence that its own procedures had gone stale — and destroying that evidence is worse than the conflict. Officer judgement is often how an organisation discovers its SOP has a gap. Both outcomes are recorded either way.

The same suspension can be triggered from the other end: if the app repeatedly reports that it followed the SOP instead of a particular judgement, that judgement is flagged for the same review. Repeated collisions are data.

Part four · How it gets used

Chapter 16Choosing what the app sees on the next case

A new claim arrives. The app works out the case's labels, then goes looking for judgements that apply.

Which judgements apply

The test is strict and mechanical: a judgement applies only if every label in its scope is present on this case. A judgement scoped to theft + no police report applies to a case carrying both of those labels among its six. A judgement scoped to windscreen does not apply to a theft claim, ever. A judgement with no scope applies to every case.

In which order

More than a handful may qualify, so they are ranked, and there is a word budget (about a thousand words at most, usually far less) to keep the app's attention on what matters. The ranking rules, in order:

  1. Team judgements before individual ones. Corroborated experience outranks one person's view even when the individual one is more specific.
  2. More specific before more general. A judgement scoped to three labels beats one scoped to none, because it was learned from cases much more like this one.
  3. Better track record before worse. Judgements that have been overruled a lot rank lower.
  4. Individual judgements are capped at three, and any that duplicate a team judgement are dropped.

What the app is actually told

The selected judgements are handed over with an explicit statement of authority at the top. Not implied — written out:

Your RULES (the SOP) are SUPREME: a judgement can never override a rule. If a judgement conflicts with a rule, FOLLOW THE RULE and cite the judgement with relation "overrode_by_rule".

Each judgement then appears with its standing attached — "(team judgement — 3 officers)" or "(one officer's judgement — not yet corroborated by the team; weigh it and verify against the record before relying on it)" — so the app knows how much weight each one deserves. If any judgement for this case is suspended over an SOP conflict, a line says so.

Alongside the judgements, a small number of recent individual corrections on comparable cases ride along as raw context. These are ranked by comparability — how much they look like this case — rather than by recency. That detail matters: during a monsoon, a flood of complaint corrections would otherwise push the one relevant theft lesson out of view purely by arriving more recently.

When the app wants more than the sentence

A judgement arrives as one sentence plus its standing. That is deliberately all of it — enough to decide with, and cheap enough to put on every case. But sometimes the sentence is not enough: the app is looking at an unusual file and would do better if it knew what the officers were actually looking at when they taught this.

So the evidence is available as a tool the app can call, named lookup_judgement, rather than being loaded onto every case in advance. Given a judgement's id, it returns:

  • what the judgement applies to, and who stands behind it — including any officers who have gone against it;
  • how often it has been put to work, and how often it survived the officer's review;
  • and the cases themselves: what each officer wrote, what the app had recommended at the time, which fields they changed, and the record as it stood at that moment.

It is the same evidence a supervisor sees when they tap a judgement on the memory screen. The app simply gets to ask for it.

Why a tool instead of just including it

Because including it would drown the thing it is meant to support. Full evidence for a handful of judgements is far bigger than the recommendation itself, and most cases never need it — the sentence settles them. Loading it every time would spend the app's attention on history at the exact moment it should be reading this file.

The rule we designed to: enough in front of it to make the right call, with more available if it decides it needs to learn. On an ordinary case the app never asks, and that case costs nothing extra.

Chapter 17Finding out whether it actually helped

Every judgement put in front of the app is recorded on that decision. The app is also required to say which ones it actually used, and how:

The app saysMeaning
appliedI used this judgement and it shaped my recommendation.
overruledI saw this judgement and deliberately went against it, for a reason I state.
overrode_by_ruleThis judgement conflicted with the SOP, so I followed the SOP.

Now, when an officer rejects that recommendation, we can be precise about blame. Only the judgements the app said it used take the blame. A judgement that was merely available, or that the app explicitly overruled, is untouched. Blaming everything on screen would slowly poison every judgement in the app regardless of whether it had anything to do with the mistake.

From that, each judgement carries a simple track record: how often it was used, how often it was blamed, and the resulting accuracy. That number is deliberately withheld until a judgement has been used at least ten times — before that, it is shown as no figure at all rather than a meaningless "100% after one use".

Two more honesty rules in this area:

  • If the app stops naming the judgements it used, blame lands on nothing, and a silent drift would follow. So the rate of rejections that cite nothing is monitored as an alarm in its own right.
  • The home screen shows the memory's overall benefit — how much more often recommendations are accepted when judgements applied than when none did. If there are fewer than ten decided cases on either side of that comparison, the figure is suppressed rather than estimated. A confident number computed from four cases is worse than no number.
Part five · Living with it

The one controlled measurement

In July 2026 we ran the loop as an experiment rather than a demo, on a lending app built against a synthetic Indian retail credit policy. Nineteen DSA-sourced applications were each run twice, everything identical except whether a single judgement was switched on:

"For DSA-sourced files, verify employment directly with the employer rather than relying solely on the submitted documents."

It was formed from corrections by three named credit officers, and it appears in no policy document — so any change in behaviour cannot have come from the app reading the policy corpus. It is scoped to exactly one case label, sourcing_channel: dsa, because that is the only label all three contributing corrections had in common.

What we looked atResult
Judgement used — memory on vs off14 cases vs 1
Chance of a swing that size by luckp = 0.0005 (sign test)
Fired on the cases it was built for19 of 19 DSA files
Stayed silent on cases it was not0 of 2 non-DSA files
Approve-or-decline verdicts changednone

That last row is the honest one. Fourteen of the nineteen files were already failing a hard policy rule, so no verdict could have changed. What changed was the reasoning — an extra step nobody had written down.

The result worth trusting is the null one

Four judgements were seeded, not one. Three of them merely restated the SOP, and the app can read the SOP for itself. Retired, they changed nothing: the system reached the same conclusions without them. They fired, they were cited, and they added no value.

Memory pays off where the written procedure is silent, and close to nowhere else. Any vendor can tell you their system learns. Fewer will tell you which of their own features measurably does nothing.

Scope of the claim: one judgement, one app, nineteen cases, one environment. It shows that a learned judgement changes how the system reasons where the written policy is silent, and stays quiet where it is not. It is not evidence that decision memory helps in general, and should not be presented as such.

Chapter 18The screens and the controls

The decision card

App Memory is the curator's screen. The card is the officer's — the recommendation, its reasoning, the proposed change and the buttons. Two parts of it exist because of the memory, and one part exists to keep it honest.

What your team has taught. Sitting above the proposed change: the judgements that were put in front of the app for this case. Each shows its sentence, how many officers stand behind it, and what the app did with it — "the agent used this", "the agent went against this", or "available, not cited". The middle one matters most. A judgement the app overrode is the single most useful thing on the screen when it happens, so it is called out in its own colour rather than listed quietly with the others.

This case. A read-only strip of the case's labels, so an officer about to correct the app can see which kinds of case their correction will end up teaching.

Who decided, and when

When the officer applies a change, the record gains a decided-by and a decided-at. Neither comes from the app's proposal. Both are stamped by the platform at the moment of the write: the officer's identity from their verified session, and the server's own clock. Whatever the proposal said for those two fields is overwritten, and the override is logged. On the applied card they are marked recorded by Citra, so an officer can tell a server fact from a model suggestion.

Why the app is not allowed to fill these in

Because it filled them in badly. Left as ordinary fields for the app to propose, the model offered "Credit Officer Assistant" as the deciding officer — the name of nobody — and a decision date three weeks before the officer clicked. Both were written to a credit file, where those two columns are the audit trail.

The date is wrong even when the model is being honest: the app proposes long before a person approves, so any time it could name is a time at which nothing happened. A client that sends its own decided-by now has it replaced rather than accepted, which is what makes the who on a decision unforgeable rather than merely stated.

App Memory

The main screen, under Admin. The card that opens it shows a live summary — for example, "2 judgements from 6 corrections". Inside there are three tabs.

Judgements it has learned. Every judgement, its sentence, its case-label chips, its standing ("team · 3 officers"), and its track record. Tapping one opens the evidence: the actual corrections that taught it, with each officer's name, timestamp and words. This is the answer to "why does it think that?" — and it is one tap away.

From that panel a curator can:

  • Retire a judgement — it stops being used immediately. The evidence stays, so if the team keeps teaching the same lesson, it can form again. Retiring takes two deliberate taps.
  • Quarantine it — a reversible hold. This exists for a specific scenario: an officer leaves under a cloud and you want everything they taught held pending review, without destroying anything.
  • Resolve an SOP conflict — the two buttons from chapter 15.

Notice what is not there: there is no edit button. Judgements are never rewritten by hand, for the reasons in chapter 12.

Past decisions. Every photo and document the app has reviewed, what it suggested, and what the team decided. Read-only. A curator can exclude a particular past item from being used as an example, but the record itself is never altered or deleted.

How it's doing. The loop's health: how often officers change the app's recommendation, how often decisions turn out right, how much is handled automatically, and a plain summary of everything learned so far.

Learning Batch

The job's control panel, described in chapter 9: running or paused, how often it checks, when it last ran, how many corrections are waiting and for which app, plus Pause and "Fold feedback now".

Export

The whole memory — judgements, corrections, decision records — can be downloaded as a file, or pushed to the customer's own storage bucket. If any part of it cannot be read, the export is labelled partial and says which part failed, rather than quietly handing over an incomplete file that looks complete.

Chapter 20Eight weeks in a claims team — the whole thing as a story

Everything above, in sequence, on one team.

Week 1. The app goes live for motor claims. It has an SOP to follow and no judgements at all. Its recommendations are decent and occasionally miss things an experienced officer would catch.

Week 1, Tuesday. Priya rejects a recommendation to approve a ₹38 lakh theft claim on a three-week-old policy with no police report. She picks the category "evidence insufficient" and writes: "theft claims this size need the police report number on file before approval, especially on a policy this new." Her screen returns instantly; nothing else visible happens.

Week 1, Tuesday, six hours later. The batch wakes. Priya's is the only correction in its group, but the oldest-waiting clock has passed six hours, so the group is ready. It passes the substance gate comfortably, names nobody, and the sentence is written. Its scope comes out as theft, that amount band, and police report absent — the labels her case carried. The SOP check finds no contradiction. It goes live as an individual judgement.

Week 1, Wednesday. A similar claim arrives. The app now sees Priya's judgement, labelled as one officer's uncorroborated view, and recommends requesting the police report first. Suresh, reviewing it, agrees and approves the recommendation. Nothing is recorded as support — agreement never counts.

Week 3. Suresh and then Asha each hit cases where the app went the other way, and each corrects it in the same direction with their own wording. The batch recognises both as the same lesson and adds their names. Three distinct officers now: it becomes a team judgement, and the label the app sees changes from "one officer's judgement" to "team judgement — 3 officers". Priya's original sentence is untouched.

Week 4. A new joiner rejects three recommendations writing only "as discussed" each time. Those cluster together and are refused for lack of substance. Nothing is deleted; the memory screen now shows "3 corrections were too brief to learn from — ask officers for one concrete sentence", and their supervisor has a two-minute conversation about it.

Week 5. Someone writes "Mr Sharma's claims are always inflated." The sentence is refused, rewritten once as a pattern about repeat claimants with prior findings, and that version passes. Nobody's name enters the memory.

Week 6. The regulator relaxes the police-report requirement below a certain value and the SOP is updated. On its next reinforcement, the judgement is checked against the new SOP, found to contradict it, and immediately suspended — on that day, without anyone noticing the collision first. The app follows the new SOP. The supervisor opens it, sees the conflict explained, and taps "SOP is right — retire it."

Week 7. A different judgement, about fleet policies, has been used fourteen times and overruled six. Its accuracy is now visible — it was measured only once it passed ten uses — and it ranks below better performers when both apply.

Week 8. A supervisor is asked in an audit why a particular claim was flagged. She opens App Memory, taps the judgement, and shows the three officer corrections with names, dates and words that produced it. That question, under the old memory, had no answer at all.

Chapter 21Questions people ask

Does the app change its own behaviour without anyone approving?

It changes what it recommends. It never changes what it is allowed to do. Learning never widens the app's autonomy; a human still approves the same things they approved on day one.

Can one difficult officer teach the app something wrong?

They can create an individual judgement, which will be labelled as one person's view, capped so it cannot crowd out corroborated ones, measured from its first use, visible on screen with their name on it, and retirable in two taps. What they cannot do is create a rule, or override the SOP.

Are some officers trusted more than others?

No. There are no per-officer trust weights, deliberately. Scoring people would need a fairness story we cannot honestly tell, and it would make the memory a performance-management tool, which it must never be. What counts is how many distinct officers taught a lesson, not who they were.

What if the source system starts using a category nobody declared?

Those cases get an "unknown" label, which is counted and surfaced as drift, and which can never be used to scope a judgement. That is how you find out the upstream system changed.

What happens if a builder renames a reason category?

The old name is kept as an alias, so corrections from before and after the rename still count as the same lesson. A rename is not allowed to split one lesson in two.

An officer was dismissed. Can we remove everything they taught?

Yes — filter judgements by who taught them and quarantine each one. Quarantine is a reversible hold, not a deletion, so if the review clears them nothing has been destroyed.

What if officers just approve everything under time pressure?

Then the app learns nothing, because only corrections teach. This is a real limitation and we state it plainly: software cannot tell agreement from fatigue. What we do is surface review velocity so a supervisor can see it, and never treat approvals as evidence.

Does memory get shared between customers?

No. Every deployment is single-tenant, and judgements are scoped to one app in one organisation. Nothing learned in one customer's system is visible to another.

Does pausing learning break anything?

No. Corrections keep being recorded, the app keeps working, and when you resume, everything accumulated is folded in. Pausing is lossless by design.

Why did my correction not become a judgement?

One of four reasons, all visible: not enough evidence yet, too brief to carry a lesson, it named a person, or the officers in that group were pulling in opposite directions. In every case the correction is still on file and still counts toward a future lesson.

The promises

Five properties that hold today. If a proposed change would break one, that is a reason to stop and rethink the change.

  1. A judgement's words are written once and never rewritten. Later feedback adds names, standing and statistics — never words.
  2. Evidence is append-only. Refusal, retirement and quarantine hide things; nothing deletes them.
  3. The SOP outranks everything learned, both when the app decides and when a judgement is created. Conflicts are surfaced for a human, never resolved silently, in either direction.
  4. Only disagreement teaches. Approvals never count as support.
  5. No per-officer trust weights, and learning never widens autonomy. A human still approves.
